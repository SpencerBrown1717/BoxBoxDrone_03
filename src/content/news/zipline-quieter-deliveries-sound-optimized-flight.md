On October 27, Zipline said it had rolled out a software change, "sound-optimized dynamic flying," across its delivery fleet. The company, based in South San Francisco, said each aircraft now uses an onboard sound model, plus live wind, altitude and speed, to choose a quieter path in and out of wing-borne flight. Axios returned to the same upgrade on November 4, in a wider report on noise complaints around drone delivery.

The figures are Zipline's, from what it calls internal modeling, compared with its 2025 fleet. It reports a 68 percent cut in overall sound exposure per flight and a 24 percent drop in peak volume. It also says the change shortens the average flight by 53 seconds, leaves the aircraft on the wing about 50 percent longer, and reduces energy use per flight by 21 percent. Those numbers have not been presented here as an independent measurement.

## Why the software can go quieter

Zipline's argument starts with airframe design. Most small delivery drones have to keep several motors spinning to stay up. Zipline says its aircraft has a fixed wing, so once it reaches cruise it can shut down all but one motor and fly "on-wing." The new software, it says, makes that transition more efficient. The drone reads the wind with its weather sensors, then uses the sound model to pick a quieter way to get onto the wing or back off it: dipping the nose to pick up speed, or pitching up to slow down, rather than doing the same work with a louder burst of motor.

Keenan Wyrobek, Zipline's co-founder and chief technology officer, said the company wants people "to love having Zipline available, not just tolerate it." The release also points to earlier work: patented propellers, and a two-part setup that, Zipline says, keeps the aircraft about 330 feet up during a delivery, while a droid lowers the package, compared with an average of 32 feet for other delivery drone services. Those comparisons are the company's.

## Noise is becoming a constraint

Axios placed the upgrade in a harder context. It reported that Amazon had withdrawn plans for a drone delivery hub in Westborough, Massachusetts, after residents and lawmakers raised concerns about noise, safety and privacy. The same article said Alphabet's Wing uses low-pitch propellers and a tethered drop, and that a new DoorDash delivery drone uses six slower propellers intended to make less noise. Axios also warned that quieter is not the same as quiet, especially as flights multiply over the same streets.

## What to watch

Zipline is presenting a software change, not a new aircraft, and the headline reductions come from its own model. The useful test is whether neighborhoods and regulators treat the quieter flights as a real difference, and whether independent measurements agree with the 68 percent and 24 percent figures. Delivery companies that want doors they have never seen also have to be accepted above the houses they already fly over.
